# Blue Fit: tips and tricks (draft)

Longer explanations behind the app's checks and tips, for a future "Tips and tricks" page in the app.
Written in plain words for lifters. Each tip says how strong the evidence is: **research**, **coaching practice**
(what experienced coaches and lifters do), or **our call** (the app's own reasoning).

---

## Every angle

**What it means:** many muscles have more than one part, or do more than one job. To grow the whole muscle, train
each part or each movement at least once a week. It doesn't have to be every workout: across the week is enough.

**Examples:**
- **Back:** lats (pulldowns, straight-arm pulldowns), upper back (rows), lower back (hip hinges, back extensions).
- **Hamstrings:** they bend the knee (leg curls) and straighten the hip (hip hinges). Leg curls alone train only the
  knee end.
- **Biceps:** long head (arm behind the body, like an incline curl), short head (arm in front, like a preacher curl),
  and the brachialis under it (hammer curls).
- **Triceps:** the long head crosses the shoulder too, so it's trained best with the arm overhead; pushdowns hit the
  other two heads well.
- **Quads:** the rectus femoris crosses the hip too; squats and leg presses train it little, leg extensions train it
  well.

**What's a hip hinge?** Bending forward at the hips with a straight back, then standing back up. The Romanian deadlift
is the classic one. It trains the hamstrings from the hip end and stretches them under load.

### Why it matters

**1. Muscles don't grow evenly.** A muscle isn't one block that grows the same everywhere. Each exercise loads some
parts more than others, and those parts grow more. This is called *regional hypertrophy*. One exercise, done forever,
leaves some parts behind.

**2. Muscles that cross two joints need two kinds of work.** Some muscle parts cross two joints (the hamstrings cross
the hip and the knee; the rectus femoris crosses the hip and the knee; the triceps long head and biceps long head cross
the shoulder and the elbow). How stretched they are depends on *both* joints. An exercise that bends only one joint
often leaves them short and slack, so they work less and grow less.

**3. What the studies show:**
- **Quads:** in a 12-week study, people who only squatted didn't grow the rectus femoris meaningfully, while people who
  rotated squats with other leg exercises (including leg extensions) grew all four quad muscles (Fonseca 2014).
- **Hamstrings:** a hip exercise (45° hip extension) grew the long head of the biceps femoris more than Nordic curls, a
  knee exercise (Bourne 2017). Leg curls with the hips bent (seated) grew the hamstrings more than lying leg curls
  (Maeo 2021).
- **Triceps:** overhead extensions grew the long head clearly more than the same exercise with the arm at the side
  (Maeo 2023).
- **Overall:** a 2022 review found that some planned variety in exercises improves regional growth and strength, but
  random, constant switching can hurt progress (Kassiano 2022).

**4. Benefits beyond size:**
- **A fuller look:** the whole muscle grows, not one bulge with flat spots.
- **Stronger in more positions:** strength is partly specific to the movement and angle you train.
- **Sturdier joints and fewer strains:** for example, hamstrings strong at the hip end matter for sprinting and
  bending, where most hamstring strains happen.
- **Less wear:** spreading the work over a few movements is easier on joints than repeating one pattern.

**How to do it well:** pick one or two exercises per angle and keep them for a while (several weeks), so you can
progress on them. Variety means covering the angles, not changing exercises every workout.

**Evidence:** research (small studies, but they point the same way).
- [Kassiano et al. 2022: "Does Varying Resistance Exercises Promote Superior Muscle Hypertrophy and Strength Gains? A Systematic Review" (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438660/)
- [Fonseca et al. 2014: "Changes in exercises are more effective than in loading schemes to improve muscle strength" (PDF)](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Changes%20in%20exercises%20are%20more%20effective%20than%20in%20loading%20schemes%20to%20improve%20muscle%20strength.pdf)
- [Bourne et al. 2017: Nordic hamstring vs hip extension, hamstring size (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27660368/)
- [Maeo et al. 2023: triceps overhead vs neutral arm position (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35819335/)
- Maeo et al. 2021: seated vs lying leg curls, hamstring growth (Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise)

## Sets per workout: 11 or fewer per muscle

**What it means:** in one workout, give a muscle up to about 11 sets. Sets that hit it only partly count as half
(for example, rows count half for the biceps). If a muscle needs more sets than that in a week, split them over two
days.

**Why:** extra sets in the same workout add less and less. In the largest analysis so far, muscle growth stopped
improving in a meaningful way after about 11 such sets per workout.

**In the app:** 12 or 13 sets shows a yellow warning (a little over, not a big deal). 14 or more shows red.

**Evidence:** research, still a preprint (not yet peer reviewed). The authors note there's little data on very high
set counts. The yellow range is our call.
- [Remmert, Pelland, Robinson, Hinson, Zourdos 2025: "Is There Too Much of a Good Thing? Meta-Regressions of the Effect of Per-Session Volume on Hypertrophy and Strength" (SportRxiv)](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)
- [FAU news release on that study](https://fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/build-muscle-gain-strength-study.php)
- [Pelland et al. 2024: weekly volume and frequency (SportRxiv)](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460): more sets per week grow more muscle, with smaller gains as you add more.

## Heavy and light

**What it means:** give each muscle some heavy work (10 reps or fewer) and some light work (12 reps or more), not
only one kind. A common setup: heavier on the big lifts, lighter on isolation exercises.

**Is it needed for growth?** Not really. Muscles grow about the same anywhere from roughly 6 to 30 reps, as long as
each set ends close to failure.

**Why do it anyway:**
- easier on joints and recovery (not everything heavy)
- heavy sets build strength better
- variety keeps training from going stale
- it's what most coaches and experienced lifters do

**What about muscle fibers?** The idea: heavy weights train the fast, strong fibers and light weights the slow,
endurance fibers, so mixing grows everything. In practice, when a light set goes close to failure, the body brings in
the fast fibers too as you tire, so both kinds of sets end up training all the fibers. A few studies hint that light
sets grow the slow fibers a bit more and heavy sets the fast ones, but a review on exactly this called it possible,
not proven (Grgic & Schoenfeld 2018). Fibers are a minor reason at best.

**In the app:** always a yellow warning when missing, never red. The 10 and 12 rep cut-offs are our call.

**Evidence:** research (growth is similar across rep ranges) + coaching practice (mixing).
- [Schoenfeld, Grgic, Van Every, Plotkin 2021: "Loading Recommendations for Muscle Strength, Hypertrophy, and Local Endurance: A Re-Examination of the Repetition Continuum" (Sports)](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/9/2/32/xml)
- [Stronger By Science: the "hypertrophy range"](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/hypertrophy-range/)
- [Built With Science: high reps vs low reps](https://builtwithscience.com/high-reps-vs-low-reps)
- [Boxrox: benefits of combining rep ranges](https://www.boxrox.com/?p=200032)
- [Speediance: mixed rep ranges](https://www.speediance.com/blogs/strength-programming/mixed-rep-ranges-workout)

## Leg extension: seat back

**What it means:** set the backrest all the way back and lean back against it, so your hips are more open (bent about
40° instead of about 90° sitting upright).

**Why:** the rectus femoris, the front-of-thigh muscle that also crosses the hip, gets stretched more when you lean
back. In a 10-week study, overall quad growth was the same either way, but the rectus femoris grew much more with the
seat back (about 170% more). The actual gain was a few millimeters, but it adds up over years.

**Sitting upright isn't wrong:** it still trains the quads well, just with less stretch on that one muscle.

**Evidence:** research (one study, summarized by Jeff Nippard).
- [Jeff Nippard: "A Better Way To Do Leg Extensions" (YouTube)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ztNBgrGy6FQ)

## Building bigger quads: what matters and what doesn't

Common advice, checked against the research:

- **Narrow stance to target the outer quad:** doesn't really work. Stance width barely changes how hard the quads
  work; a wide stance mostly adds adductors and glutes. You can't aim a squat at one part of the quad. Use the stance
  that feels best.
- **Going deeper:** in a squat-depth study (Kubo 2019), deep squats grew the glutes and adductors more, while the
  quads grew about the same as with half squats. If you want quads without much glute, stopping around parallel is
  fine.
- **Feet forward or heels raised on a Smith squat:** lets the knees travel further, so the quads get more stretch
  without much extra glute work.
- **Toes pointed in on leg extensions:** weak, mixed evidence. Costs nothing to try a small turn.
- **Bulgarian split squats:** great exercise, but they work the glutes hard, so skip them if your goal is less glute.
- **Adductors:** worth training. They add a lot of thigh thickness seen from the front.
- **Weekly sets and tempo:** about 10–15 hard sets a week for quads over two days, and a slow, controlled lowering
  (about 3 seconds), is a solid setup.

**Evidence:** research where named; the rest is coaching practice.
